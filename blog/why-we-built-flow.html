Why we built Flow.

In September 2024, Google killed SMTP authentication for Gmail. We watched clients pay Zapier $240 a year to send a single email. So we wrote the replacement.

  2. 02. SMTP, quietly dying

This is the origin story of Flow, our visual workflow automation plugin for Kintone. It starts with a single frustrated Slack message from a client in Manila and ends, eighteen months later, with a production system handling thousands of emails a day across three continents.

01. The $240 email

In late 2024, one of our largest Kintone clients — a logistics operation in Manila — sent a message to their operations channel that said, verbatim: "ang mahal naman nung nag-eemail lang tayo." Roughly: "This is too expensive just for sending emails."

What they were paying for was a Zapier Team subscription. Twenty dollars a month. The only thing it did was bridge Kintone records to Gmail. A delivery confirmation would be auto-generated in Kintone; Zapier would detect the new record; Zapier would format an email; Zapier would send it via their Gmail integration. Ninety dollars per quarter, all for one email per delivery.

The math is awful. Zapier Team is $20/month at the time of writing. Over a year that's $240. Over ten years at a growing company, it's thousands. For one webhook that sends one email.

But the client wasn't stupid — they were stuck. Because Kintone alone could not reliably send an email to the customer after September 2024. And that's where the real story starts.

02. SMTP, quietly dying

For as long as Kintone has existed, the simplest way to send an email from a record was a snippet of JavaScript running in the browser. You'd put Gmail SMTP credentials in the script (or, more responsibly, in a proxy your team owned), and the record would fire off an email on create. Hundreds of Kintone customizations around the world did exactly this.

Then Google turned it off.

The specific change was "less secure apps" — Google's name for password-based SMTP/IMAP access. In stages through 2022–2024, Google deprecated and then removed the ability to sign into Gmail with a plaintext password. By September 2024 it was fully gone. Any script still trying to authenticate with a Google password got a 535 error and was kicked out.

Microsoft announced the same for Exchange Online and Outlook.com. The deadline they gave was April 2026. We're now past it.

The replacement is OAuth 2.0. Which is correct — OAuth is genuinely more secure — but it is a wildly different programming model. Where SMTP was "paste a password in a config file," OAuth is "register an application with Google, configure consent screens, request scopes, handle redirect URIs, store refresh tokens encrypted at rest, build a token refresh loop, handle revocation, support token rotation."

OAuth is correct. OAuth is also a tax on every small team that used to just send an email.

03. OAuth is a tax

Imagine you're a Kintone consultant. A client says: "when a purchase order is approved, email the supplier the PDF." That used to be a fifteen-line JavaScript file. Today, to do it properly, you need:

  • 01 —A Google Cloud project, with the Gmail API enabled.
  • 02 —An OAuth consent screen configured with your app name and scopes.
  • 03 —A backend service with a stable HTTPS URL to receive the OAuth callback, because Kintone's plugin sandbox can't be a redirect URI.
  • 04 —A place to store the user's refresh token, encrypted at rest, scoped per-subdomain so that OAuth granted on one client's Kintone doesn't leak to another.
  • 05 —A token refresh loop to obtain new access tokens before they expire every hour.
  • 06 —And then — finally — the email-sending code itself, which is about ten lines.

Items 01 through 05 are easily two weeks of engineering for someone who has never done them before. Multiply that by every Kintone consulting shop in the world that's been hit with this change, and you start to see the scale of the problem.

Zapier solved it by centralizing. You don't register your own OAuth app — you use Zapier's. You don't host your own token refresh loop — they do. You pay $20/month and the SMTP-is-dead problem just goes away. And that's exactly what our Manila client, and tens of thousands of others, did.

But Zapier's price compounds. Add a second automation, you need a higher tier. Add team members, you need yet a higher tier. At 2,000 tasks per month you're at $50+. At 10,000 you're well north of $100. For a Kintone team whose entire interest is sending one email per record to a supplier, this is extortionate.

04. What we actually built

Flow replaces Zapier for this one thing — and only for Kintone. That narrow scope is the whole advantage.

We registered one OAuth app per provider. One on Google Cloud, one on Azure. We built the token storage (encrypted, per-subdomain, in a Kintone app that the customer themselves owns). We hosted the OAuth callback on Cloud Run in Tokyo. We wrote the token refresh loop. We wrote the email sender. We built the visual trigger editor inside the Kintone plugin itself, so customers never leave the Kintone UI.

Then we priced it at $49/month. Slightly below Zapier's entry tier. Unlimited automations, unlimited users on the subdomain, unlimited emails per month. Because our marginal cost per email is approximately $0.00007 — OAuth emails are essentially free — there was no reason to meter it.

The engineering took six months. Token refresh bugs, Kintone iframe quirks, Microsoft's "Send As" permission model, the Gmail API's surprisingly aggressive rate limits on cold tokens. Nothing especially hard, but lots of it. The plugin itself went through thirty-four versions before we'd call it done. It is, at the time of writing, at v4.9.3 and shipping in production.

05. The lesson

Every time a platform makes a security change, a tax appears. The change is correct; the tax is unintended.

In 2022, when Google started deprecating "less secure apps," the right response from Kintone's customization ecosystem would have been collective action — a shared OAuth app, a shared token store, a shared plugin. That didn't happen, because the ecosystem is fragmented and each consulting shop had its own more urgent work. So Zapier ate the opportunity, and Kintone customers paid the bill.

We built Flow because we realized we were one of the consulting shops that should have built it three years ago. It's not a heroic origin story. It's a slightly embarrassed one.

But here it is. Flow ships. If you've been paying Zapier to send email from Kintone, stop. Install Flow. Spend the savings on your team.
